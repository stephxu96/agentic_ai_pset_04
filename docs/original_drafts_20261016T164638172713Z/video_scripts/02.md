# A CAD File Is Not Yet an Engineering Workflow

## Scene 1 — The answer first

**Narration:** Which CAD-agent claims withstand scrutiny? A Backflip STEP export passed two mass checks after import into Fusion. But native-feature transfer was partial, and a multibody test failed. That is evidence for a narrow neutral-file outcome, not a dependable autonomous engineering workflow. This video uses AI-generated presentation. As of October 16, 2026.

**Visual:** Split screen. Left: STEP file → Fusion → “Mass check: passed.” Right: native feature tree and seven-body shelf → “Not established.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c1–02-c4, 02-c11` Engineering.com, “Welcome to the vibe-CAD era,” September 30, 2026.

## Scene 2 — What the mass checks actually checked

**Narration:** Engineering.com reported two tests using simplified TooTallToby drawing exercises. In the first, it imported a Backflip STEP file into Fusion, applied material, checked mass, and found the result exactly correct. In the second, the downloaded STEP file also passed its mass check. These are reported facts. The check observed imported neutral geometry producing the expected mass under the selected material in Fusion.

**Visual:** Two labeled test cards. Each shows: “Backflip STEP export” → “Fusion import” → “apply material” → “mass check: passed.” A highlighted label reads: “Observed: mass after import.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c1, 02-c2` Engineering.com, “Welcome to the vibe-CAD era,” September 30, 2026.

## Scene 3 — What those checks did not establish

**Narration:** A mass check is not a check of editable design intent. It does not by itself check dimensional tolerances, assembly relationships, fabrication feasibility, or inspection results. That is not a criticism of the test. It is its scope. My interpretation is simple: passing mass after STEP import is artifact evidence. It does not establish native editability, tolerance conformance, or manufacturability.

**Visual:** A checked-versus-unchecked table. Checked: “Mass after STEP import.” Not checked: “Feature history,” “tolerances,” “manufacturability,” “physical inspection.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c11` Engineering.com, “Welcome to the vibe-CAD era,” September 30, 2026; MIT-CDFG, “On the Opportunities and Risks of Frontier Models for 3D Modeling, Computational Design and Robotics,” September 22, 2026.

## Scene 4 — The counterchecks

**Narration:** Engineering.com also reported tests that checked different capabilities. In its first drawing-to-CAD trial, Backflip’s Fusion add-in only partially recreated geometry. In a multibody wire-shelf test, it created one of seven bodies. These are reported facts. Their checks concern native import behavior and multibody handling. They show why a successful STEP export and a successful editable reconstruction are separate claims.

**Visual:** Two panels. Panel one: partial Fusion feature tree, labeled “Observed: partial geometry recreation.” Panel two: seven outlined shelf bodies, with one filled, labeled “Observed: 1 of 7 bodies.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c3, 02-c4` Engineering.com, “Welcome to the vibe-CAD era,” September 30, 2026.

## Scene 5 — The wider evidence record

**Narration:** MIT-CDFG says it reviewed 1,646 public posts across 707 cases. It also says only 132 cases include runnable code, and that most listed capabilities are demonstrations rather than independent reproductions. Those are the survey’s claims about its record, not independent validation of every listed result. More importantly, MIT-CDFG says tolerance specifications and manufacturability have not been tested on physical parts in the CAD evidence it summarizes.

**Visual:** Survey record graphic: “1,646 public posts,” “707 cases,” “132 with runnable code.” Below, a physical part icon with a red label: “Tolerance and manufacturability: not tested on physical parts.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c5, 02-c6, 02-c7` MIT-CDFG, “On the Opportunities and Risks of Frontier Models for 3D Modeling, Computational Design and Robotics,” September 22, 2026.

## Scene 6 — A supporting robotics warning

**Narration:** Robotics offers a supporting warning about aggregate scores. The Physical AI Podcast reports that 63 RoboDojo tasks went unsolved by all evaluated models. It also reports that its two leading models shared only eight successes. These are podcast source claims from automatic transcripts, so the numbers require checking against the underlying study and audio before publication. The narrower interpretation still matters: one aggregate score can hide distinct failure profiles.

**Visual:** Robot-task grid. “63 unsolved by all.” Two model columns with only eight overlapping check marks. Footer: “Transcript figures need audio and study verification.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c8, 02-c9` Physical AI Podcast, “AI Models Fail 63 of 84 Robot Tasks, Waymo Borrows $5B,” October 10, 2026, 00:02:47–00:02:51 and 00:03:48–00:03:55.

## Scene 7 — Define the output being tested

**Narration:** A second podcast makes the same point in another domain. NVIDIA’s Cosmos Lab vice president describes world simulation as taking a current observation and an action input, then predicting what happens in the future. That is a source description of a capability. It is not evidence that the prediction is physically correct, or that it transfers to a real robot.

**Visual:** Diagram: “Current observation + action sequence” → “Predicted future: video, audio, or other output.” A separate dashed box reads: “Physical correctness and real-world transfer: not checked here.”

**Source on screen:** `02-c10` Changelog Media, “Open models and the future of Physical AI with NVIDIA,” October 1, 2026, 00:23:59–00:24:19.

## Scene 8 — What to do next

**Narration:** For buyers and builders, use separate acceptance gates: STEP validation, native editability, multibody behavior, tolerances, and manufacturability. For each gate, record the artifact, toolchain, material assumptions, grader, and failure cases. Watch for evaluations that test physical parts and disclose runnable methods. Until then, a passed CAD export is a useful drafting result, not a completed engineering workflow.

**Visual:** Five-gate checklist: STEP validation; native editability; multibody behavior; tolerances; manufacturability. Final card: “Ask: what was checked, by whom, and under what conditions?”

**Source on screen:** `02-c11` Engineering.com, September 30, 2026; MIT-CDFG, September 22, 2026.
