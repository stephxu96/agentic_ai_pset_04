Which CAD-agent claims withstand scrutiny? A Backflip STEP export passed two mass checks after import into Fusion. But native-feature transfer was partial, and a multibody test failed. That is evidence for a narrow neutral-file outcome, not a dependable autonomous engineering workflow. This video uses AI-generated presentation. As of October 16, 2026.

Engineering.com reported two tests using simplified TooTallToby drawing exercises. In the first, it imported a Backflip STEP file into Fusion, applied material, checked mass, and found the result exactly correct. In the second, the downloaded STEP file also passed its mass check. These are reported facts. The check observed imported neutral geometry producing the expected mass under the selected material in Fusion.

A mass check is not a check of editable design intent. It does not by itself check dimensional tolerances, assembly relationships, fabrication feasibility, or inspection results. That is not a criticism of the test. It is its scope. My interpretation is simple: passing mass after STEP import is artifact evidence. It does not establish native editability, tolerance conformance, or manufacturability.

Engineering.com also reported tests that checked different capabilities. In its first drawing-to-CAD trial, Backflip’s Fusion add-in only partially recreated geometry. In a multibody wire-shelf test, it created one of seven bodies. These are reported facts. Their checks concern native import behavior and multibody handling. They show why a successful STEP export and a successful editable reconstruction are separate claims.

MIT-CDFG says it reviewed 1,646 public posts across 707 cases. It also says only 132 cases include runnable code, and that most listed capabilities are demonstrations rather than independent reproductions. Those are the survey’s claims about its record, not independent validation of every listed result. More importantly, MIT-CDFG says tolerance specifications and manufacturability have not been tested on physical parts in the CAD evidence it summarizes.

Robotics offers a supporting warning about aggregate scores. The Physical AI Podcast reports that five frontier models were put in charge of robots across 84 Robot World tasks, and that 63 of those tasks went unsolved by any of them. It also reports that its two leading models shared only eight successes. These are podcast source claims from automatic transcripts, so the numbers require checking against the underlying study and audio before publication. The narrower interpretation still matters: one aggregate score can hide distinct failure profiles.

A second podcast makes the same point in another domain. NVIDIA’s Cosmos Lab vice president describes world simulation as taking a current observation and an action input, then predicting what happens in the future. That is a source description of a capability. It is not evidence that the prediction is physically correct, or that it transfers to a real robot.

For buyers and builders, use separate acceptance gates: STEP validation, native editability, multibody behavior, tolerances, and manufacturability. For each gate, record the artifact, toolchain, material assumptions, grader, and failure cases. Watch for evaluations that test physical parts and disclose runnable methods. Until then, a passed CAD export is a useful drafting result, not a completed engineering workflow.
