# Engineering Teams Should Try AI First Where Mistakes Are Easy to Fix

**Question:** What should engineering leaders do with the newest AI tools for CAD, simulation, and product records? **Answer:** Try them first where they help people find, prepare, or review files. Do not let them change the official engineering record without a person's approval.

*As of October 16, 2026.*

Watch the video version: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=06Vi4mkEthE

Onshape, NVIDIA, and Autodesk describe three different kinds of tool. They should not be judged by the same standard.

Onshape says its AI-Generated Thumbnail Description Search is an optional Onshape Labs feature. [[1]](https://www.onshape.com/en/blog/ai-generated-thumbnail-description-search) It says the released version works only on public documents. [[1]](https://www.onshape.com/en/blog/ai-generated-thumbnail-description-search) The feature writes searchable descriptions from model preview images, so users can find documents by what they look like, not only by name or file details.

The test here is whether search can find a likely file from public previews. That matches a claim about finding things. It does not match stronger claims that the shape is correct, that two parts are equivalent, that a part can be made, or that it is fit for reuse. A bracket found by search may look right without having the right dimensions, material, tolerances, or revision.

This makes search a sensible first pilot. My interpretation is that mistakes here are easy to undo: a person can check a search result before using it. Because the released feature covers only public documents, it also shows little about performance on a company's private files. [[1]](https://www.onshape.com/en/blog/ai-generated-thumbnail-description-search) It does not show that search reduces duplicate design work in practice.

NVIDIA describes a different step: preparing a robot model for simulation. Its walkthrough says the workflow checks the imported robot's scale, parts, and appearance against reference material. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw) It says the workflow sets up joints, collision shapes, and physical properties. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw) NVIDIA says it then reruns its readiness checks and a grasp test after fixing problems. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw) The final sequence runs in Isaac Sim, NVIDIA's robot simulator. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw)

These checks go further than search. They support the claim that the model is ready for the simulation shown: it matches its references, its physical properties are set, and a simulated grasp works. They do not show that a real robot will perform reliably in the physical world. Simulation can catch real errors, but it is still a test inside Isaac Sim.

Taken together, the sources suggest a rule none states directly: adopt these tools in order of how easily mistakes can be undone and how much they cost. Start with search. Move to simulation preparation when teams can check the model and keep the results of its checks. Require a stronger approval step before an AI writes information it has inferred into the official product record.

That last step matters because Autodesk is describing a riskier tool. Autodesk executive Jeff Kinder says an AI agent for PLM (product lifecycle management, the official record of parts, revisions, and approvals) is expected to launch within the next couple of quarters. [[3]](https://www.engineering.com/autodesks-ai-push-is-actually-a-pull/) He says it would draw information from Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Outlook conversations. [[3]](https://www.engineering.com/autodesks-ai-push-is-actually-a-pull/) Kinder also says it could find PLM information in those conversations and enter it into the PLM system. [[3]](https://www.engineering.com/autodesks-ai-push-is-actually-a-pull/)

This is a claim about a planned product, not one in use. The question is not whether an AI can pull a plausible fact from a conversation. It is whether the system gets the facts, sources, revision, and approval status right, and enters them only under control. The evidence does not describe those review controls or give independent customer results compared with current practice.

Onshape's released search works only on public documents, while Autodesk describes a future tool that would put inferred information into PLM. [[1]](https://www.onshape.com/en/blog/ai-generated-thumbnail-description-search) This is not a claim that one system is more accurate. It is a recommendation to start where a wrong answer is easier to catch and undo.

Robotics adds a warning: simulation results are not evidence of real-world performance. NVIDIA's workflow includes a simulated grasp test. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw) The Physical AI Podcast reports that most cited results for world-action models, AI that predicts what will happen and chooses robot actions, came from simulation tests, and that its real-robot result covered only one task. [[4]](https://physicalaipodcast.co/episodes/009) That account is secondary reporting from an automatic transcript, so check its figures against the audio and the original paper before acting on them. Still, it suggests what to watch: results repeated on physical tasks matter more than another simulation-only demo. [[2]](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/H5UaOdIZERw)

World Labs says it has signed a definitive agreement to join AMD, subject to regulatory approval and the usual closing conditions. [[5]](https://www.worldlabs.ai/blog/amd-announcement) This is a business deal, not evidence of what the technology can do.

What should readers do next? Run a small search pilot with people checking the results. For simulation work, keep the reference material, model changes, check results, and simulation conditions. For any planned PLM automation, require an approval step that leaves a record before inferred information enters the system. Watch for published evidence from real deployments, clear review controls, and results on physical tasks, not only in simulation.
