Title
AI Now Writes Design Features and Sets Up Physics Tests

Subtitle
Two software makers say their AI tools can do this. Neither has shown that the results are correct.

Body

Question: What changed, and which developments matter most to engineering teams? Answer: AI is moving into two steps of engineering work: writing reusable CAD features and setting up simulations. That matters more than a chat window beside CAD. But the evidence so far shows product announcements, not reliable production geometry or correct engineering decisions.

As of October 16, 2026.

Watch the video version: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pICJnOCUzMc

Onshape says its FeatureScript MCP Server lets large language models (LLMs) work inside Onshape, so teams can create custom CAD features by describing them in plain language. [1] Onshape also says the requirements in a request, including manufacturing details such as tolerances, can carry through into the model. [1]

FeatureScript is Onshape's programming language for reusable CAD features whose dimensions adjust to their inputs. That makes this different from asking an AI for a single shape. A team could request a feature built to a company standard, read the generated code, try different inputs, and check whether it follows the intended design rules. My interpretation is that this is a useful direction: the AI produces something engineers can review and reuse.

But the evidence supports no more than that. The only evidence is Onshape's product description. It tells us what Onshape says the server does. It is not a published test of generated FeatureScript across requirements, unusual cases, revisions, or manufacturing results. So it does not show that a generated feature keeps the designer's intent in a production model. [1]

The second announcement goes further. Engineering.com reports that SimScale's Engineering AI Agent is available in Onshape's app store. [2] SimScale says that, from one prompt, its agent can take a CAD model, propose a setup, break the model into small cells for calculation (a mesh), run the physics, and return results in Onshape, even when the geometry is not ready for simulation. [2]

This puts AI at a riskier step. To check a generated CAD feature, you ask whether the code and geometry meet stated rules. To check a simulation setup, you must also ask whether the model answers the engineering question at all. Loads, boundary conditions, material assumptions, contact between parts, mesh choices, solver settings, and how results are read all matter. A simulation that finishes running is not proof of a valid analysis.

Engineering.com confirms only that the integration is available. [2] The SimScale release it quotes shows only what SimScale says the agent can do. [2] Neither source compares the agent's setups with setups engineers have reviewed, and neither shows that its results match the real physical system. A claim about correct simulation decisions would need those tests.

Together, the two announcements show something neither shows alone. Vendors are building AI into the steps where work passes from one engineering stage to the next, rather than offering it only as a writing assistant. My interpretation is that this changes how teams should judge these tools. For FeatureScript, reviewers check code, inputs, geometry, and design rules. For simulation, they must also check whether the simulation model fits the question. The same "one prompt" can hide very different amounts of checking.

Robotics offers context, not proof, for either tool. In an NVIDIA Robotics Office Hour, a presenter says Newton, NVIDIA's physics engine for robot simulation, is completely open source. [3] The only evidence is that statement. It supports NVIDIA's claim about who can use and contribute to Newton. It does not show that the simulator is accurate, that results carry over to real hardware, or that it helps any CAD-to-simulation workflow.

A Physical AI Podcast episode describes a blind test on real robots: 10 object-handling tasks, three attempts per task, a shared time limit, and a shared scoring sheet. [4] If confirmed, that is the kind of disciplined test engineering AI needs: common tasks, a fixed number of attempts, and one way of scoring. But the evidence is an automatic transcript of the podcast, not the test materials or a scorer anyone can audit. The figures and details remain uncertain until checked against the audio, video, and original test documents. [4]

None of these sources shows that AI-generated CAD features are safe for production or that AI-chosen simulation setups are correct.

Teams should try these tools where inputs are controlled and engineers still review the results. For CAD, require tests tied to the intended design rules and inputs. For simulation, have engineers approve the setup before anyone treats the output as a decision. Watch for public test sets, published scoring methods, repeatable comparisons with setups written by engineers, and evidence that links a simulation result to physical tests.
