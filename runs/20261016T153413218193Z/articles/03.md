# Adopt the Workflow, Not the Unchecked Output

**Question:** What do these developments imply for engineering leaders, and what should they watch next?

**Answer:** Use AI to accelerate first-draft CAD, reusable feature development, model search, and simulation preparation. Keep release decisions behind checks that match the engineering risk. A valid model, an accurate mass, or a completed simulation sequence is useful evidence—but each establishes only the property it actually checked.

*As of October 16, 2026.*

The practical mistake is to treat faster upstream generation as proof of downstream readiness. The emerging evidence supports a narrower conclusion: AI can produce useful engineering work products, but the release gate still belongs to tolerance, manufacturability, and downstream validation processes.

MIT’s survey reports that the public record does not yet show frontier-model outputs meeting engineering requirements such as tolerances or manufacturability. `03-c1` That is a reported finding about the evidence record, not proof that no model can meet those requirements. The survey also reports that tolerance specifications and manufacturability have not been tested on physical parts in the CAD evidence it reviewed. The check therefore does not match a production-readiness claim: benchmarked or demonstrated editable CAD is not a physical-part tolerance or manufacturing test.

Engineering.com offers a more concrete example of why the distinction matters. Its reporter tested Backflip’s drawing-to-CAD output by importing a STEP file into Fusion, applying material, and checking mass. `03-c3` The reporter said two tested parts matched the required mass exactly. That is a meaningful, narrow check: it tests mass after import under the chosen material assignment. It does not, by itself, establish feature-history preservation, dimensional conformance, drawing interpretation across a representative set, or manufacturability.

The same trial also found that Backflip created only one body from a seven-body drawing. `03-c2` That observed failure is useful precisely because it catches a requirement a mass check might miss. A single correct-looking body is not an assembly. The analysis adds something beyond either source alone: the right question is not whether an AI-produced file “worked,” but whether the test exposed the particular failure modes relevant to the next decision.

Onshape describes a workflow aimed at making AI useful earlier in that chain. The company says its FeatureScript MCP Server lets engineers use plain-language requests to have AI help create, test, debug, and refine custom CAD functionality. `03-c4` This is a vendor claim about its product pathway, not an independent outcome evaluation. Its stated scope is promising for reusable internal features—especially where teams can encode their own standards and review generated FeatureScript—but “test” in that description does not establish that an output meets a company’s release criteria. Engineering leaders should treat this as a workflow claim and retain code review, geometry checks, and domain-specific validation.

NVIDIA makes a parallel claim for CAD-to-simulation preparation. In its video, NVIDIA describes checking an imported robot’s scale, parts, and appearance against references. `03-c5` It also describes grasp tests and selected simulation-readiness checks before running a full sequence in Isaac Sim. `03-c6` Those are sensible checks for a simulation asset. They match claims about reference alignment, configured simulation properties, and performance of the selected sequence. They do not match claims about manufacturing release, physical hardware behavior, or robustness outside the tested setup.

That boundary is not pedantry. It is the central operating rule. My interpretation is that simulation-ready status should not be treated as manufacturing-release evidence, because the survey identifies untested physical-part tolerance and manufacturability requirements while NVIDIA describes simulation-readiness checks. `03-c8` The evidence supports separation between those gates, not a judgment that simulation preparation lacks value.

Robotics offers supporting context for this caution. The Physical AI Podcast reports that five general models left 63 of 84 RobotWorld tasks unsolved, with a program checking whether each job was completed. `03-c7` This is a source claim, and the reported check matches task completion in that program. It does not establish anything directly about CAD quality, tolerance, or production transfer. It does reinforce a broader point: systems acting through tools need outcome checks, not confidence in their intermediate plans.

Production-readiness evidence remains incomplete. Engineering.com tested a small, curated set of drawings. Onshape and NVIDIA are describing their own products and workflows, rather than publishing independent comparative evaluations. No accepted source here provides independently replicated comparisons of AI-generated CAD features or simulation-ready assets against representative production tolerances, manufacturability, or real-world transfer.

What should teams do now? Put AI where iteration is cheap and review is strong: first drafts, reusable feature development, search, conversion, and simulation setup. Define release gates explicitly and map each to its required evidence: body count and topology for assemblies; dimensions and tolerances for parts; process-specific manufacturability for production; and representative physical tests where hardware behavior matters. Watch next for disclosed evaluations that separate model output from the CAD, simulation, and review systems around it—and that state what was checked, on what inputs, and where it failed.
